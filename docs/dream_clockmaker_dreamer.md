# THE STOPPED HOUR: the dreamer and the wheels

One page, written before the build. All names are invented. Places, gears and difficulty stay; `src/data/tuning.js` is untouched and no timing window moves.

## Who dreams this

**Ansel Dunmarrow**, 74. Keeper of the town clock for forty-one years, and not one tooth slipped. A stiff left hip, a leather apron, a small brass wheel always in his hand.

**Why this hour stopped.** Six is winding time. One evening his wife Ida laid the table for six and he went up anyway, because six wants winding. The weight stuck. It took him an hour, and he could have left it: nobody looks up. Ida died at half past six, quietly, at the table. He came down the hill at seven.

So in the dream it is never seven. **The gears did not slip. He took them off himself.** If it never strikes seven, he is not late. The town waits inside his hour, and Ida is still somebody who sends a pie up at five. Nell says "slipped" because that is what you say.

## The choice

Under the bell, after "If it never gets to seven, I'm not late":

- **"Go home. I'll strike it."** He sets off down the hill as Jo strikes, each lamp lighting ahead of him. Jo is alone at the rope. By the time Jo reaches the lane he has gone in, the door is on the latch, and nobody saw it: the work nobody sees, done for the man who did it for forty-one years.
- **"Strike it with me."** He stands at the rope and counts the strokes aloud. "I never had a second man." He waits at his door for Jo: "Stand there a minute. Till I've got the door shut."

Both go on to the same orb. They differ in the yard, the green, the door, and the ending's second line.

## The ending, and what catching means

He goes in, an hour late. We never see inside; the door is warm. The orb is on his step. **Catching this dream is not winding a clock. It is letting an hour end**, the one thing he could not do with his own hands. Ending: "The clock struck seven, an hour late." / the branch line / "By morning nobody on the green could say it had ever stopped." / "Nell knew. Nell lit the last lamp." (That line already existed; it now means more: she knew they never slipped.)

## The one new action: hang a wheel

Jo picks up a real brass wheel, carries it on his back through the room, and hangs it on a peg on the wall. Wheels that touch turn each other, **each the opposite way**, and a pawl only lets the last wheel turn the way its arrow shows. So a train is right when it reaches from the driving wheel to the ratchet with the right *count* of wheels. Three wheels all touching each other lock solid. Wrong trains judder and clack; nothing is lost and any wheel lifts off again.

- **Physical, not a bar.** There is no marker and no clock. The thinking is at the wall and the work is carrying a heavy thing past what is in the way.
- **Keys.** [E] takes, hangs, lifts, and sets down. Movement is unchanged. Touch uses the existing E button and d-pad.
- **Taught safely, then tested:**
  1. **The bench** (clock house, no hazard, Ansel beside you): two wheels on the floor and shelf. One wheel reaches, and goes the wrong way. He explains. Two wheels by the other pegs: right.
  2. **The shaft** (under the pendulums): a big wheel from the floor, a small one from plank B, up the chain to plank C. Only the big wheel reaches the first peg. A knock drops what Jo is carrying to the floor below; it stays there to be fetched.
  3. **The bell chamber** (both ideas together): four wheels, one in the pendulum's swing, one on the joists, two on the gallery; nine pegs; only an even count works.

Length comes from doing this, not from distance. The dream stays gentle: the first knock is free, none takes the last heart, a train can't be failed, and the pendulum rules are unchanged.

## What is not changing

Nell stays who she is and the Evening's lines stay true: she still lights the last lamp, still says "You were the only one awake" ("She would," says Ansel). Ansel joins the Evening only if Jo saw him home in the dream (a `met.ansel` flag, not boarding the train).
